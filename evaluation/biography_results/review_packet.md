## Philip I, Landgrave of Hesse

Philip I, Landgrave of Hesse (1504–1567), nicknamed 'the Magnanimous,' was a German nobleman and champion of the Protestant Reformation, one of the most important early Protestant rulers in Germany. Despite imperfect education, he developed rapidly as a statesman. He embraced Protestantism in 1524 and helped suppress the German Peasants' War by defeating Thomas Müntzer at the Battle of Frankenhausen. Philip organized the church in Hesse according to Protestant principles, leading to the founding of the University of Marburg in 1527. He contributed to the Regensburg religious colloquy in 1541 and successfully secured imperial permission for the university. On his death, his territories were divided between his four sons.

## Robert Bunsen

Bunsen developed the Bunsen burner with his assistant Peter Desaga, creating a very hot and clean flame used in labs today. Working with Gustav Kirchhoff, he discovered two new elements: caesium in 1860 and rubidium in 1861. They invented a prototype spectroscope to study the colors of heated elements. Bunsen also did important work in photochemistry and organic arsenic chemistry, leading to effective antidotes for poisoning. His discoveries brought him wide acclaim and an award named after him.

## Emil von Behring

Emil von Behring was a German physiologist who revolutionized medicine by developing serum therapy against diphtheria and tetanus. Born in 1854, he studied medicine in Berlin and later worked with Robert Koch. In 1890, he and Kitasato Shibasaburō discovered that injecting toxins into animals could produce antitoxins to cure these diseases. After optimizing the process, successful human treatments began in 1894. Behring founded a company to produce these treatments and became a professor at the University of Marburg. He was awarded the first Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1901 for his work, earning him the title 'saviour of children' as diphtheria was a major cause of child death.

## Gertrud von Le Fort

Baroness Gertrud von Le Fort (1876–1971) was a German writer born in Minden, the daughter of a Prussian colonel. She studied in Heidelberg, Marburg, and Berlin before making her home in Bavaria. Her career began with editing Ernst Troeltsch's posthumous work in 1925, followed by her conversion to Roman Catholicism. She published over 20 books, including the novella The Song at the Scaffold, which inspired Georges Bernanos' Dialogues des Carmélites. This work formed the basis for Francis Poulenc's 1956 opera. Le Fort won the Gottfried-Keller Prize in 1952 and was nominated by Hermann Hesse for the Nobel Prize.
